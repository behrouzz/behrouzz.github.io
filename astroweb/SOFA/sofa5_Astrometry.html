SOFA: Astrometry Tools

This is the heart of the SOFA collection. Astrometry is the science of measuring positions on the sky, and the SOFA astrometry tools answer a very practical question: a star has a given entry in a catalog; in which direction do I have to point my telescope, at a given time, from a given place on the Earth? In between lie proper motion, parallax, the bending of light by the Sun, the aberration caused by the Earth's motion, precession and nutation, the rotation of the Earth and the refraction of the atmosphere. SOFA handles all of them in a rigorous and consistent way, to the level of a few microarcseconds in the best case.

The chain of transformations

The next figure shows every step between the catalog entry of a star (the first box) and the direction in which the observer sees it (the last box). Read it from left to right, row by row. Each box is a reference system, with the variables that describe a direction in it; each arrow is a correction or a transformation. The catalog variables are the ones that you give to the SOFA functions: ra, dec are rc, dc, the proper motions pm_ra, pm_dec are pr, pd, the parallax is px and the radial velocity is rv.

  • ICRS: the catalog system, fixed on the distant background. A star is described by its position, its motion and its distance.
  • BCRS: the direction of the star as seen from the barycentre of the Solar System, once its own space motion has been applied.
  • astrometric: the same, once the parallax has been applied. It is the direction in which the star would appear to an observer at the Sun's position if there were no Sun (or other bodies) to bend the light.
  • GCRS: the direction for an observer at the centre of the Earth, after light deflection and aberration.
  • CIRS: the same direction in the system of the Earth's true pole and of the Celestial Intermediate Origin (frame bias, precession and nutation applied).
  • TIRS and ITRS: the direction in the Earth-fixed system, first with respect to the instantaneous rotation pole and then with respect to the terrestrial reference pole (polar motion). Here the coordinates are a longitude and a latitude on the Earth.
  • local apparent, topocentric, observed: the direction for the observer at his or her place (hour angle and declination, or azimuth and altitude), first without and then with the bending of the light by the atmosphere.

Not every application needs the whole chain. A space telescope needs only the upper part (up to GCRS). A radio interferometer may stop at the topocentric direction, because radio waves are hardly refracted. SOFA lets you use exactly the part that you need.

Quick start

Often three functions are enough. Remember that all the angles in pyerfa are in radians.

From catalog to CIRS: atci13

ri, di, eo = erfa.atci13(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, date1, date2)
  • rc, dc: ICRS right ascension and declination at the catalog epoch (radians).
  • pr, pd: proper motions in radians per Julian year. Warning: pr is the rate of change of the coordinate \(\alpha\) itself, \(d\alpha/dt\), and not \(\mu_{\alpha*} = \mu_\alpha\cos\delta\), the "motion on the sky" that you find in Hipparcos, Gaia and SIMBAD. If the catalog gives \(\mu_{\alpha*}\), divide it by \(\cos\delta\).
  • px: parallax in arcseconds. rv: radial velocity in km/s.
  • date1, date2: the date as a two-part Julian date in TT.
  • Returns the CIRS right ascension and declination ri, di (radians) and the equation of the origins eo. The classical geocentric apparent place is \((ri - eo,\ di)\).

From CIRS to observed: atio13

aob, zob, hob, dob, rob = erfa.atio13(ri, di, utc1, utc2, dut1, elong, phi, hm,
                                      xp, yp, phpa, tc, rh, wl)
  • utc1, utc2: the UTC, encoded with dtf2d (see the chapter about time). dut1: UT1−UTC in seconds.
  • elong, phi, hm: the observer's east longitude, geodetic latitude (radians) and height above the WGS84 ellipsoid (meters).
  • xp, yp: polar motion (radians). In low-accuracy work you can use zero.
  • phpa, tc, rh: the pressure in hectopascals (= millibars), the temperature in Celsius and the relative humidity (0 to 1, where 1 means 100%) at the observer. wl: the wavelength of observation in micrometers; a value above 100 means radio.
  • Returns the observed azimuth aob (north through east), zenith distance zob, hour angle hob, declination dob and right ascension rob. They are different ways to write the same direction.

Everything in one call: atco13

aob, zob, hob, dob, rob, eo = erfa.atco13(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, utc1, utc2, dut1,
                                          elong, phi, hm, xp, yp, phpa, tc, rh, wl)

It does the whole job, from the catalog entry to the observed direction. Each of the three functions has an inverse: atic13 (CIRS to astrometric ICRS), atoi13 (observed to CIRS) and atoc13 (observed to ICRS). For the simple conversions between the hour angle \(h\) and declination and the azimuth and elevation there are also hd2ae and ae2hd (and hd2pa for the parallactic angle).

A few remarks. The pressure should be the real pressure at the place of the observer, not the pressure reduced to sea level (the "QNH" of pilots) that weather reports give. Use the "QFE": at an altitude of 625 m the pressure is about 950 hPa, not 1013. The result is given as zenith distance, not altitude, to avoid any confusion about which horizon is meant. And when the input is UTC, always encode it with dtf2d (never as an ordinary Julian date), so that leap seconds are correctly handled.

The three kinds of coordinates

  • Catalog coordinates are meant to be valid for a long time, so they are given in a system fixed on the distant background (ICRS) together with the motion of the star. SOFA supports only the ICRS as the catalog system. Older catalogs used mean equator and equinox of 1950 or 2000: for these, there are the functions fk45z, fk425, fk52h etc. (see the last chapter).
  • Current coordinates are the ones for the date of observation, independent of the observer's location: the CIRS \((\alpha, \delta)\) (or the classical apparent place). In this system the hour angle is \(h = \theta + \lambda - \alpha\) where \(\theta\) is the Earth rotation angle and \(\lambda\) the east longitude.
  • Observed coordinates are the direction in which a perfect telescope has to be pointed in order to see the star, including refraction.

Design of the SOFA astrometry functions

The functions are designed according to four principles: (1) the most common transformations should need only one call; (2) the best accuracy should be available; (3) but without making things hard for the user; (4) efficiency matters, but must not complicate simple cases.

Two stages: context and transformation

The calculation is naturally divided in two. Some parameters do not depend on the star at all: the date, the position and velocity of the Earth, the precession-nutation matrix, the rotation of the Earth, the position of the observer and the weather. These are collected in a record called the context (named ASTROM in SOFA; in pyerfa it is a NumPy structured value). Heavy computations, like the 1365-term nutation series, are done only once, when the context is filled. After that, each star is transformed quickly, using the context. If you have thousands of stars, you build the context once; if you have one star, the one-call functions do both stages for you.

Algorithms and accuracy

It is useful to know what each step does and how well it does it.

Time scales

Several functions ask for the TDB. You can safely give TT instead: the difference is less than 2 ms, which has no effect on aberration or precession. But UTC and TT differ by more than a minute, and the difference matters whenever the accuracy has to be better than a milliarcsecond. For the observed place, the functions also need UT1 (the time of the Earth's rotation), so you must know UT1−UTC from the IERS. This is the most important number to get right when you compute an observed place (for the pointing of a telescope): without it, the error can be up to about 13 arcseconds.

Space motion and parallax: pmpx

The star moves in a straight line with constant speed. Combined with the Earth's position (parallax), this gives the direction of the star in the barycentric system at the time of observation. The function is also tolerant of unphysical data (for example, a very small parallax with a large proper motion). It includes a small correction for the Roemer effect (the change of the light travel time). Stars whose parallax is zero or negative, or those that have a very high speed, can be treated with the safer pmsafe, and starpm is a more rigorous one.

Light deflection: ld, ldsun, ldn

According to general relativity, light passing near a massive body is bent. For a star, the deflection by the Sun is up to 1.75 arcseconds at the limb of the Sun. The function ldsun computes this effect for the Sun alone and is accurate to about a microarcsecond over most of the sky. For the planets (for example, a star near Jupiter) one uses ldn, with the mass and the position of each body, or ld for one body at a time. The effect of Jupiter can reach 16 mas at its limb.

Aberration: ab

Because the Earth moves at about 30 km/s, a star appears shifted in the direction of the Earth's motion, by up to about 20 arcseconds. This is the aberration of light. ab uses the velocity of the observer, with the correct relativistic formula. If the velocity of the observer (Earth's centre plus the rotation of the Earth) is given, the whole aberration is computed in one go; with the velocity of the geocentre only you get the annual aberration, and the diurnal aberration (up to 0.3 arcsecond) has to be added later.

Earth attitude and rotation

The functions with names ending in 13 use the IAU 2006/2000A models for precession-nutation. They have an accuracy of about 1 mas, limited by the free core nutation, which cannot be modelled. For best results, use the lower-level functions (apci, apco) and give them the CIP coordinates \(X, Y\) with the corrections published by the IERS (as in Example 2 of the previous chapter).

The observer on the Earth: pvtob

This function gives the position and the velocity of the observer in the CIRS. The WGS84 ellipsoid is always used (other ellipsoids make too small a difference to matter). Polar motion is included.

Refraction: refco

The atmosphere bends the light, so a star appears higher than it really is. There is no perfect model for this. SOFA uses the classical expression

$$\Delta z = A\tan z + B\tan^3 z$$

where \(z\) is the zenith distance, with special care near the horizon. The constants \(A\) and \(B\) come from refco, which takes the pressure, temperature, humidity and wavelength. The model is better than 10 mas (RMS) for visible light and better than 50 mas for radio waves, down to \(z = 75^\circ\). We can look at how large the effect is:

import numpy as np
import erfa

DD2R, DR2D, DAS2R, DMAS2R = erfa.DD2R, erfa.DR2D, erfa.DAS2R, erfa.DMAS2R

# standard conditions: 1013.25 hPa, 15 C, 50 % humidity, visible light
A, B = erfa.refco(1013.25, 15.0, 0.5, 0.55)
print(f"A = {A / DAS2R:.3f} arcsec, B = {B / DAS2R:.4f} arcsec")

for z in (30, 45, 60, 75, 85):
    t = np.tan(np.radians(z))
    print(f"zenith distance {z:2d} deg: refraction = {(A * t + B * t**3) / DAS2R:6.1f} arcsec")
A = 57.176 arcsec, B = -0.0654 arcsec
zenith distance 30 deg: refraction =   33.0 arcsec
zenith distance 45 deg: refraction =   57.1 arcsec
zenith distance 60 deg: refraction =   98.7 arcsec
zenith distance 75 deg: refraction =  210.0 arcsec
zenith distance 85 deg: refraction =  555.8 arcsec

At 45° from the zenith, the refraction is about one arcminute, and it grows quickly near the horizon, where it reaches about half a degree. This is why even the best refraction models limit the accuracy of any observed place.

How accurate is it?

  • Everything that involves refraction is limited by the refraction model.
  • Without refraction, the precession-nutation model limits the accuracy to about 1 mas.
  • The deflection by the Sun is accurate to 1 μas over most of the sky. Near the Sun it may reach 0.5 mas, and close to a planet other bodies may matter (use ldn).
  • The Earth ephemeris epv00 limits the aberration to about 5 μas.
  • Each transformation and its inverse agree to better than 1 μas (if there is no refraction).

Example 1: from catalog to observed place

As the first complete example we take the star Gliese 555 (Hipparcos number 71253; SIMBAD name "BD-11 3759"). It is also the star used in Example 3 below, there with slightly different (fictitious) data. The catalog data from SIMBAD are:

ra              218.57004859310788   deg
dec             -12.519559582093887  deg
pmra            -355.138             mas/yr
pmdec            593.04              mas/yr
plx_value        159.9225            mas
rvz_radvel        -1.28              km/s

We observe it from Strasbourg, France, on 10 February 2024 at 22:32:37 UTC. The Earth orientation parameters come from the IERS. First, we put all the numbers in the units that SOFA wants. Two details need attention. SIMBAD's pmra is \(\mu_\alpha\cos\delta\), so we divide it by \(\cos\delta\). And the parallax must be in arcseconds.

# --- the star ---------------------------------------------------------------
rc = 218.57004859310788 * DD2R              # right ascension (rad)
dc = -12.519559582093887 * DD2R             # declination (rad)
pr = -355.138 * DMAS2R / np.cos(dc)         # d(alpha)/dt: pmra is mu_alpha * cos(dec)
pd = 593.04 * DMAS2R
px = 159.9225 * 0.001                       # mas -> arcsec
rv = -1.28                                  # km/s

# --- the time ---------------------------------------------------------------
utc1, utc2 = erfa.dtf2d("UTC", 2024, 2, 10, 22, 32, 37.0)

# --- Earth orientation parameters (IERS) ----------------------------------
dut1 = 0.00261476                           # UT1-UTC (s)
xp = 0.0473476 * DAS2R                      # polar motion
yp = 0.2368623 * DAS2R

# --- the observing site -----------------------------------------------------
elong = 7.744083817548831 * DD2R            # east longitude
phi = 48.58313582900411 * DD2R              # geodetic latitude
hm = 140.0                                  # height (m)
phpa = 987.0                                # pressure (hPa)
tc = 15.0                                   # temperature (C)
rh = 0.92                                   # relative humidity (fraction)
wl = 0.55                                   # wavelength (micrometers)

aob, zob, hob, dob, rob, eo = erfa.atco13(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, utc1, utc2, dut1,
                                          elong, phi, hm, xp, yp, phpa, tc, rh, wl)

print(f"Azimuth                 : {aob * DR2D:.6f} deg")
print(f"Zenith distance         : {zob * DR2D:.6f} deg")
print(f"Hour angle              : {hob * DR2D:.6f} deg")
print(f"Declination             : {dob * DR2D:.6f} deg")
print(f"Right ascension (CIO)   : {rob * DR2D:.6f} deg")
print(f"Equation of the origins : {eo * DR2D:.6f} deg")
Azimuth                 : 96.595528 deg
Zenith distance         : 100.899352 deg
Hour angle              : -92.377729 deg
Declination             : -12.498271 deg
Right ascension (CIO)   : 218.471414 deg
Equation of the origins : -0.307959 deg

The zenith distance is larger than 90°: the star is below the horizon at that moment, so it cannot be seen from Strasbourg. The azimuth of 96.6° means that it is just south of east (below the horizon) and the hour angle of −92° (about −6.2 hours) tells us it has not yet culminated: it will cross the meridian in about six hours, and rise before that.

Example 2: the same star, step by step (to CIRS)

When we need the position in the CIRS system and not the observed place, we use atci13. We can also do it in two stages, with the context. First apci13 fills a context for the date (in TT), then atciq transforms the star with it. The results are identical:

# UTC -> TAI -> TT
tai1, tai2 = erfa.utctai(utc1, utc2)
tt1, tt2 = erfa.taitt(tai1, tai2)

# in one call
ri, di, eo = erfa.atci13(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, tt1, tt2)
print(f"atci13        : {ri * DR2D:.9f}  {di * DR2D:.9f}")

# in two stages: context first, then the star
astrom, eo = erfa.apci13(tt1, tt2)
ri2, di2 = erfa.atciq(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, astrom)
print(f"apci13+atciq  : {ri2 * DR2D:.9f}  {di2 * DR2D:.9f}")
atci13        : 218.586845398  -12.622083532
apci13+atciq  : 218.586845398  -12.622083532

The CIRS position is not far from the catalog position (218.57°, −12.52°). The difference of about 0.017° in right ascension and 0.1° in declination comes mostly from 24 years of precession (up to about 20 arcseconds per year), plus nutation, aberration and the proper motion of the star. The context astrom can be reused for any number of stars, as we show next.

Example 3: the SOFA cookbook example, in full

This long example from the SOFA documentation goes through all the steps of the chain, in a variety of ways. Its aim is to show how the different functions are related. All the values are artificial, although realistic. The site is at latitude 15° 57' 42.8" S, longitude 5° 41' 54.2" W, at 625 m. The star is at ICRS (14h 34m 16.81183s, −12° 31' 10.3965") with proper motions of −354.45 and +595.35 mas/yr, a parallax of 0.16499" and a null radial velocity. The date is 2013 April 2, 23h 15m 43.55s UTC. The Earth orientation parameters are: polar motion \(x_p = 50.995\) mas, \(y_p = 376.723\) mas, UT1−UTC = 155.0675 ms, and the corrections to the CIP, \(dX = 0.269\) mas and \(dY = -0.274\) mas.

First, the setup. The helper function reprd prints a position in hours/degrees to the same format as the C example:

def reprd(label, ra, dec):
    """Print right ascension (h m s) and declination (d m s)."""
    sign, t = erfa.a2tf(7, ra)
    sign2, d = erfa.a2af(6, dec)
    print(f"{label:>26s} {t['h']:02d} {t['m']:02d} {t['s']:02d}.{t['f']:07d} "
          f"{sign2.decode()}{d['h']:02d} {d['m']:02d} {d['s']:02d}.{d['f']:06d}")

# site
elong = erfa.af2a("-", 5, 41, 54.2)
phi = erfa.af2a("-", 15, 57, 42.8)
hm = 625.0

# weather and wavelength
phpa, tc, rh, wl = 952.0, 18.5, 0.83, 0.55

# times: UTC, then TT
utc1, utc2 = erfa.dtf2d("UTC", 2013, 4, 2, 23, 15, 43.55)
tai1, tai2 = erfa.utctai(utc1, utc2)
tt1, tt2 = erfa.taitt(tai1, tai2)

# Earth orientation parameters
xp = 50.995e-3 * DAS2R
yp = 376.723e-3 * DAS2R
dut1 = 155.0675e-3
dx = 0.269e-3 * DAS2R
dy = -0.274e-3 * DAS2R

# the star
rc = erfa.tf2a("+", 14, 34, 16.81183)
dc = erfa.af2a("-", 12, 31, 10.3965)
pr = np.arctan2(-354.45e-3 * DAS2R, np.cos(dc))     # mu_alpha* -> d(alpha)/dt
pd = 595.35e-3 * DAS2R
px = 164.99e-3
rv = 0.0

reprd("ICRS, epoch J2000.0:", rc, dc)
      ICRS, epoch J2000.0: 14 34 16.8118300 -12 31 10.396500

Step 1: the catalog position is changed to an astrometric place with atcc13. This removes the space motion and the parallax, and puts the star on the celestial sphere, as if it were a distant object in the same direction on that date. That is the quantity to compare with the J2000.0 position at the top: by 2013 the proper motion moved the star by some seconds of arc.

rca, dca = erfa.atcc13(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, tt1, tt2)
reprd("catalog -> astrometric:", rca, dca)
   catalog -> astrometric: 14 34 16.4960283 -12 31 02.523786

Step 2: the astrometric place to CIRS with atci13, giving the star zero proper motion and parallax (the effects are already included). Then, the catalog position is moved directly to CIRS. The two results must be the same:

ri, di, eo = erfa.atci13(rca, dca, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, tt1, tt2)
reprd("astrometric -> CIRS:", ri, di)

ri, di, eo = erfa.atci13(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, tt1, tt2)
reprd("catalog -> CIRS:", ri, di)
      astrometric -> CIRS: 14 34 20.2370587 -12 34 36.381654
          catalog -> CIRS: 14 34 20.2370587 -12 34 36.381654

Step 3: the classical (equinox-based) geocentric apparent place is obtained from CIRS with the equation of the origins: \(\alpha_{app} = \alpha_{CIRS} - EO\). The declination is the same in both systems.

ra = erfa.anp(ri - eo)
da = di
reprd("geocentric apparent:", ra, da)
      geocentric apparent: 14 35 01.7725802 -12 34 36.381654

Step 4: from CIRS to the observer's sky with atio13. Giving zero for the pressure switches off refraction and gives the topocentric position (what an observer would see without an atmosphere). With the weather data, we get the observed position.

# topocentric (no refraction: zero pressure, temperature, humidity and wavelength)
aot, zot, hot, dot, rot = erfa.atio13(ri, di, utc1, utc2, dut1, elong, phi, hm,
                                      xp, yp, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0)
reprd("CIRS -> topocentric:", rot, dot)

# observed (with refraction)
aob, zob, hob, dob, rob = erfa.atio13(ri, di, utc1, utc2, dut1, elong, phi, hm,
                                      xp, yp, phpa, tc, rh, wl)
reprd("CIRS -> observed:", rob, dob)
      CIRS -> topocentric: 14 34 20.2570287 -12 34 36.141207
         CIRS -> observed: 14 34 16.9649101 -12 34 44.643091

Step 5: the whole transformation from the catalog to the observed place in a single call, atco13. The result is slightly different from the two-stage route (by a few microarcseconds). The reason is that the two-stage route first computes a geocentric position and adds diurnal effects later in a way that is not perfectly rigorous. The direct function includes the observer's position and velocity in the aberration and parallax from the beginning, and so it is more rigorous:

aob, zob, hob, dob, rob, eo = erfa.atco13(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, utc1, utc2, dut1,
                                          elong, phi, hm, xp, yp, phpa, tc, rh, wl)
reprd("ICRS -> observed:", rob, dob)
         ICRS -> observed: 14 34 16.9649106 -12 34 44.643094

Step 6: using our own data in the context. We now go back to ICRS → CIRS, but this time we use better data than the standard models: the position and the velocity of the Earth from the JPL ephemeris DE405, and the CIP from the IERS corrections. We start from the SOFA ephemeris epv00 (we need the heliocentric position anyway), then overwrite the barycentric position and velocity with the JPL values. The CIP is computed from the IAU 2000A matrix and the corrections \(dX, dY\) are added, then s06 gives the locator \(s\). With apci we fill the context, and with atciq we transform the star:

pvh, pvb = erfa.epv00(tt1, tt2)        # SOFA ephemeris of the Earth (heliocentric and barycentric)

# replace the barycentric position and velocity by JPL DE405 values (au, au/day)
pvb = pvb.copy()
pvb["p"] = [-0.9741704366519668, -0.2115201000882231, -0.0917583114068277]
pvb["v"] = [0.0036436589347388, -0.0154287318503146, -0.0066892203821059]

# CIP from the IAU 2000A model, plus the corrections from the IERS
rnpb = erfa.pnm00a(tt1, tt2)
x, y = erfa.bpn2xy(rnpb)
x, y = x + dx, y + dy
s = erfa.s06(tt1, tt2, x, y)

# fill the context, and use it
astrom = erfa.apci(tt1, tt2, pvb, pvh["p"], x, y, s)
ri, di = erfa.atciq(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, astrom)
reprd("ICRS -> CIRS (JPL, IERS):", ri, di)
 ICRS -> CIRS (JPL, IERS): 14 34 20.2370639 -12 34 36.381756

The result differs from the standard one by about 0.1 milliarcsecond. Almost all of the difference comes from the IERS corrections to the CIP, which mostly describe the free core nutation that the standard model leaves out. The better Earth velocity contributes at most 5 μas.

Step 7: light deflection by the planets too. So far only the Sun deflected the light. With atciqn we can add more bodies. For each body we need its mass (in solar masses), a limiting angle (dl, inside which the calculation is switched off to avoid numerical trouble), and its barycentric position and velocity at the time of the observation (here again from DE405). The order is the order in which light passes the bodies: Saturn, Jupiter and the Sun. In pyerfa the bodies are a NumPy array with the special type erfa.dt_eraLDBODY:

bodies = np.zeros(3, dtype=erfa.dt_eraLDBODY)

bodies["bm"] = [0.00028574, 0.00095435, 1.0]        # masses in solar masses
bodies["dl"] = [3e-10, 3e-9, 6e-6]                  # deflection limiters
bodies["pv"]["p"] = [[-7.8101442680818964, -5.6095668114887358, -1.9807981923749924],
                     [0.7380987962351833, 4.6365869247538951, 1.9693136030111202],
                     [-0.0007121743770509, -0.0023047830339257, -0.0010586596574639]]
bodies["pv"]["v"] = [[0.0030723248971152, -0.0040699547707598, -0.0018133584165345],
                     [-0.0075581692172088, 0.0012691372216750, 0.0007279990012801],
                     [0.0000062923521264, -0.0000003308883872, -0.0000002964866231]]

ri, di = erfa.atciqn(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, astrom, bodies)
reprd("ICRS -> CIRS (+ planets):", ri, di)

The change is only about 40 microarcseconds, mainly due to Saturn, which in this fictitious case is 0.38° from the star, while Jupiter and the Sun are about 150° away.

Step 8: as a final check, we return from CIRS to the astrometric place with the inverse function aticqn. It agrees with what we had in Step 1:

rca2, dca2 = erfa.aticqn(ri, di, astrom, bodies)
reprd("CIRS -> astrometric:", rca2, dca2)
      CIRS -> astrometric: 14 34 16.4960283 -12 31 02.523786

Example 4: many stars, one context

Observatories transform lists of thousands of stars. The big saving is to fill the context once with apco13 (it is star-independent), then use atciq and atioq for the stars. Because pyerfa functions accept arrays, we can even transform all the stars in a single call. We use three objects: the star of Example 3, the fictitious star of Example 5 below, and an imaginary very distant source with zero proper motion and parallax. All are observed with the conditions of Example 3.

# the context for the site, the date and the weather of Example 3 (done only once)
astrom, eo = erfa.apco13(utc1, utc2, dut1, elong, phi, hm, xp, yp, phpa, tc, rh, wl)

# three stars: ICRS ra, dec (rad); pr, pd (rad/yr); parallax (arcsec); radial velocity (km/s)
stars_rc = np.array([rc, 353.22987757 * DD2R, 180.0 * DD2R])
stars_dc = np.array([dc, 52.27730247 * DD2R, 30.0 * DD2R])
stars_pr = np.array([pr, np.arctan2(22.9 * DMAS2R, np.cos(stars_dc[1])), 0.0])
stars_pd = np.array([pd, -2.1 * DMAS2R, 0.0])
stars_px = np.array([px, 0.023, 0.0])
stars_rv = np.array([rv, 25.0, 0.0])

# all three at once: ICRS -> CIRS -> observed
ri, di = erfa.atciq(stars_rc, stars_dc, stars_pr, stars_pd, stars_px, stars_rv, astrom)
aob, zob, hob, dob, rob = erfa.atioq(ri, di, astrom)

for i in range(3):
    print(f"star {i + 1}: azimuth = {aob[i] * DR2D:9.5f} deg, zenith distance = {zob[i] * DR2D:9.5f} deg")
star 1: azimuth =  91.39161 deg, zenith distance =  42.75004 deg
star 2: azimuth =   1.34603 deg, zenith distance = 143.55440 deg
star 3: azimuth =   6.59167 deg, zenith distance =  46.17372 deg

The first object is the star of Example 3, at zenith distance 42.75°, so it is well above the horizon. A zenith distance larger than 90°, as for the second object, means that the object is below the horizon; the numbers are then just its direction as if the Earth were transparent. The same code would work with a million stars.

Updating only the Earth's rotation

A telescope control system needs a stream of positions, say once a second, for tracking. Almost everything in the context changes slowly, but the Earth rotates 15° per hour. The function aper13 updates only the Earth rotation angle in the context. It is not completely rigorous, because other things also change, but the neglected changes are small: in an hour, the diurnal aberration changes by up to 80 mas, the annual aberration by 15 mas, and the precession by 6 mas. Large telescopes rarely recompute the whole context more often than once a minute. Let us advance one hour and compare with a full computation:

rc, dc = stars_rc[0], stars_dc[0]
pr, pd, px, rv = stars_pr[0], stars_pd[0], stars_px[0], stars_rv[0]
ri, di = erfa.atciq(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, astrom)           # CIRS position, done once

# one hour later: update only the Earth rotation angle in the context
utc1b, utc2b = erfa.dtf2d("UTC", 2013, 4, 3, 0, 15, 43.55)
ut11, ut12 = erfa.utcut1(utc1b, utc2b, dut1)
astrom_later = erfa.aper13(ut11, ut12, astrom)

quick = erfa.atioq(ri, di, astrom_later)
full = erfa.atco13(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, utc1b, utc2b, dut1,
                   elong, phi, hm, xp, yp, phpa, tc, rh, wl)

print(f"azimuth:           quick {quick[0] * DR2D:.6f}  full {full[0] * DR2D:.6f} deg")
print(f"zenith distance:   quick {quick[1] * DR2D:.6f}  full {full[1] * DR2D:.6f} deg")
print(f"difference in azimuth: {abs(quick[0] - full[0]) / DAS2R:.3f} arcsec")
azimuth:           quick 86.936369  full 86.936386 deg
zenith distance:   quick 28.299189  full 28.299203 deg
difference in azimuth: 0.058 arcsec

The difference is only a small fraction of an arcsecond, much less than the accuracy a telescope needs for pointing.

Example 5: the long way, step by step

The one-call functions hide the physics. To really understand the chain, we now calculate the observed direction of a (fictitious) star with the individual building blocks of SOFA: pmpx (proper motion and parallax), ldsun (deflection by the Sun), ab (aberration), the bias-precession-nutation matrix, and atio13 (for the Earth rotation, polar motion, diurnal effects and refraction). We follow the recipe published by Patrick Wallace in his "SOFA example PDF". All of it is done with vectors, using the tools of the Vector-Matrix library. The input data are:

  • The observing site: longitude 9.712156° E, latitude 52.385639° N, height 200 m. The time of observation is 2003 August 26, 00:37:38.973810 UTC.
  • The star (a fictitious Tycho-2 star, epoch 2000): \((\alpha, \delta) = (353.22987757^\circ, +52.27730247^\circ)\), proper motions \(\mu_\alpha\cos\delta = +22.9\) mas/yr and \(\mu_\delta = -2.1\) mas/yr, parallax 23 mas and radial velocity +25 km/s.
  • The Earth orientation parameters from the IERS: UT1−UTC = −0.349535 s, corrections to the CIP \(dX = +0.038\), \(dY = -0.118\) mas, polar motion \(x_p = 0.259371''\), \(y_p = 0.415573''\).
# star
rc = 353.22987757 * DD2R
dc = 52.27730247 * DD2R
pr = np.arctan2(22.9 * DMAS2R, np.cos(dc))      # mu_alpha* -> d(alpha)/dt
pd = -2.1 * DMAS2R
px = 23.0 * 0.001
rv = 25.0

# time of observation (UTC -> TT)
utc1, utc2 = erfa.dtf2d("UTC", 2003, 8, 26, 0, 37, 38.973810)
tai1, tai2 = erfa.utctai(utc1, utc2)
tt1, tt2 = erfa.taitt(tai1, tai2)

# Earth orientation parameters
dut1 = -0.349535
xp = 0.259371 * DAS2R
yp = 0.415573 * DAS2R
dx = 0.038 * DMAS2R
dy = -0.118 * DMAS2R

# a small helper: print a unit vector as right ascension and declination in degrees
def print_sph(p, text):
    w, d = erfa.c2s(p)
    print(f"{text}: ra = {erfa.anp(w) * DR2D:.8f}, dec = {d * DR2D:.8f}")

The bias-precession-nutation matrix. We start with the matrix of the IAU 2006/2000A models, pnm06a. From it we extract the position of the CIP, add the IERS corrections to \(X\) and \(Y\), compute the CIO locator \(s\) for the corrected pole, and build a more accurate GCRS-to-CIRS matrix with c2ixys:

r = erfa.pnm06a(tt1, tt2)
x, y = erfa.bpn2xy(r)
x, y = x + dx, y + dy
s = erfa.s06(tt1, tt2, x, y)

bpn = erfa.c2ixys(x, y, s)       # GCRS -> CIRS

The Earth's position and velocity. We need the heliocentric and the barycentric position and velocity of the Earth (epv00). From them we calculate a few quantities used later: the distance from the Sun to the observer \(em\) (in au) and the unit vector \(eh\) from the Sun to the observer, which is needed for the light deflection; the barycentric velocity \(v\) of the observer in units of the speed of light (the light travel time for 1 au is AULT seconds, and a day has DAYSEC seconds); and the reciprocal of the Lorentz factor \(bm1 = \sqrt{1-v^2}\), needed for aberration:

pvh, pvb = erfa.epv00(tt1, tt2)

em, eh = erfa.pn(pvh["p"])                      # Sun-observer distance (au) and unit vector

v = erfa.sxp(erfa.AULT / erfa.DAYSEC, pvb["v"]) # barycentric velocity in units of c
v2 = np.dot(v, v)
bm1 = np.sqrt(1.0 - v2)                         # 1 / Lorentz factor

Up to here nothing depends on the star. Now we follow the light.

1. Proper motion and parallax. The function pmpx moves the star from the catalog epoch to the date of observation and applies the parallax with the position of the Earth. The time is counted in Julian years from J2000.0. The result is the direction of the star in the barycentric system (the "coordinate direction"):

pmt = ((tt1 - erfa.DJ00) + tt2) / erfa.DJY      # time since J2000.0 in Julian years
pco = erfa.pmpx(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, pmt, pvb["p"])
print_sph(pco, "BCRS (after proper motion and parallax)")
BCRS (after proper motion and parallax): ra = 353.22991889, dec = 52.27730584

2. Light deflection by the Sun. The Sun bends the light of every star a little, even far from the solar disc: here the change is about 2.5 milliarcseconds. The result is the "natural direction":

pnat = erfa.ldsun(pco, eh, em)
print_sph(pnat, "BCRS (after light deflection)     ")
BCRS (after light deflection)     : ra = 353.22991848, dec = 52.27730518

3. Aberration. It shifts the direction by about 18 arcseconds on the sky, as we can see below. The result is the "proper direction" and it is the GCRS position of the star:

ppr = erfa.ab(pnat, v, em, bm1)
print_sph(ppr, "GCRS (after aberration)            ")
GCRS (after aberration)            : ra = 353.23789321, dec = 52.27695263

4. Bias, precession and nutation. Multiplying with the matrix moves the star from the GCRS to the CIRS. We get the CIRS right ascension and declination:

pi_vec = erfa.rxp(bpn, ppr)
print_sph(pi_vec, "CIRS                              ")

w, di = erfa.c2s(pi_vec)
ri = erfa.anp(w)
CIRS                              : ra = 353.23300209, dec = 52.29554174

5. From CIRS to the observer. What remains (Earth rotation, polar motion, diurnal aberration and parallax, refraction) is the work of one function, atio13. If we give it zero atmospheric parameters, we get the topocentric direction. If we give the weather data (pressure, temperature, humidity, wavelength), we get the observed direction:

elong = 9.712156 * DD2R
phi = 52.385639 * DD2R
hm = 200.0
phpa = 1000.0       # pressure (hPa)
tc = 20.0           # temperature (C)
rh = 0.70           # relative humidity
wl = 0.55           # wavelength (micrometers)

# topocentric: no atmosphere
aot, zot, hot, dot, rot = erfa.atio13(ri, di, utc1, utc2, dut1, elong, phi, hm, xp, yp,
                                      0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0)
print(f"Topocentric: hour angle = {hot * DR2D:.8f}, dec = {dot * DR2D:.8f}")
print(f"Topocentric: azimuth = {aot * DR2D:.8f}, altitude = {90 - zot * DR2D:.8f}")

# observed: with refraction
aob, zob, hob, dob, rob = erfa.atio13(ri, di, utc1, utc2, dut1, elong, phi, hm, xp, yp,
                                      phpa, tc, rh, wl)
print(f"Observed   : azimuth = {aob * DR2D:.8f}, altitude = {90 - zob * DR2D:.8f}")
Topocentric: hour angle = -0.29507963, dec = 52.29549063
Topocentric: azimuth = 116.44983888, altitude = 89.79843387
Observed   : azimuth = 116.44983888, altitude = 89.79848800

The star is close to the zenith (altitude 89.8°), so the refraction raises it by only 0.00005° (0.2 arcseconds). Finally, as a check, we compute the same thing with the single call atco13. The small difference comes from the CIP corrections, which the single call does not apply, and from the more rigorous treatment of the diurnal effects. Note that the azimuth is extremely sensitive when the star is so close to the zenith: 25 mas in azimuth is less than 0.1 mas on the sky.

aob2, zob2, hob2, dob2, rob2, eo2 = erfa.atco13(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, utc1, utc2, dut1,
                                                elong, phi, hm, xp, yp, phpa, tc, rh, wl)
print(f"atco13     : azimuth = {aob2 * DR2D:.8f}, altitude = {90 - zob2 * DR2D:.8f}")
print(f"difference : {abs(aob - aob2) / DMAS2R:.2f} mas in azimuth, {abs(zob - zob2) / DMAS2R:.2f} mas in zenith distance")
atco13     : azimuth = 116.44984579, altitude = 89.79848802
difference : 24.88 mas in azimuth, 0.06 mas in zenith distance

Moving the star: proper motion over long times

The straight-line proper motion in pmpx is exact only for a short time. If you want to move a catalog entry to another epoch (without going through the whole chain), use pmsafe. It also behaves well with stars that have no (or negative) parallax. The more rigorous function is starpm. For example: where is the star of Example 3 one hundred years after J2000.0?

# the star of Example 3 again
rc = erfa.tf2a("+", 14, 34, 16.81183)
dc = erfa.af2a("-", 12, 31, 10.3965)
pr = np.arctan2(-354.45e-3 * DAS2R, np.cos(dc))
pd = 595.35e-3 * DAS2R
px, rv = 164.99e-3, 0.0

ep1 = (2451545.0, 0.0)                 # J2000.0
ep2 = (2451545.0 + 36525.0, 0.0)       # J2100.0
ra2, dec2, pr2, pd2, px2, rv2 = erfa.pmsafe(rc, dc, pr, pd, px, rv, *ep1, *ep2)

print(f"shift in ra  * cos(dec): {(ra2 - rc) * np.cos(dc) / DAS2R:8.2f} arcsec")
print(f"shift in dec           : {(dec2 - dc) / DAS2R:8.2f} arcsec")
shift in ra  * cos(dec):   -35.44 arcsec
shift in dec           :    59.54 arcsec

In 100 years the star moves by about 35″ towards smaller right ascension (measured on the sky) and by about 60″ north, consistent with the proper motion of \((-354, +595)\) mas/yr.

Example 6: from the sky to a photographic plate (gnomonic projection)

To draw a small piece of the sky, or to measure positions on a photograph, we need a map projection. For small fields the gnomonic (or tangent-plane) projection is the natural one: it is exactly the geometry of a pinhole camera, and also very close to the one of classical long-focus telescopes. A point of the sky with \((\alpha, \delta)\) is projected on the plane that is tangent to the sphere at the tangent point (the plate centre). The coordinates on this plane are the standard coordinates \((\xi, \eta)\): the \(\eta\) axis points to the north, the \(\xi\) axis to the east, and near the tangent point they are measured in radians. Multiplying by the focal length gives the position on the plate in the same unit as the focal length.

Real plate reduction adds more (the tilt of the plate, optical distortion, refraction), but the pure geometry is the basis. SOFA gives six functions in three pairs (one for spherical coordinates, one for vectors):

You knowYou wantFunction
star + tangent pointstandard coordinates \(\xi, \eta\)tpxes (tpxev for vectors)
\(\xi, \eta\) + tangent pointstartpsts (tpstv)
\(\xi, \eta\) + startangent pointtpors (tporv)

Let us try this: an astrograph with a focal length of 3430 mm and a photographic plate 120 mm wide, pointing at an observed position close to the north celestial pole: (9h 00m 00s, +89° 30' 00"). This is a hard case, chosen to test the rigor of the algorithms. What are the plate coordinates of a star at (5h 00m 00s, +89° 45' 00")?

fl = 3430.0                                   # focal length (mm)

raz, decz = 9.0 * 15.0 * DD2R, 89.5 * DD2R    # tangent point (radians)
ra, dec = 5.0 * 15.0 * DD2R, 89.75 * DD2R     # the star

xi, eta = erfa.tpxes(ra, dec, raz, decz)
print(f"image [x, y] / mm = {xi * fl:+8.3f}, {eta * fl:+8.3f}")
image [x, y] / mm =  -12.961,  +22.450

The other way around: what star is at the plate position (−20, +60) mm?

x, y = -20.0, 60.0
ra, dec = erfa.tpsts(x / fl, y / fl, raz, decz)

sign, t = erfa.a2tf(2, ra)
sign, d = erfa.a2af(1, dec)
print(f"star [RA, Dec] = {t['h']:02d} {t['m']:02d} {t['s']:02d}.{t['f']:02d}  "
      f"{sign.decode()}{d['h']:02d} {d['m']:02d} {d['s']:02d}.{d['f']}")
star [RA, Dec] = 23 14 31.74  +89 23 48.8

Finally, if we know the position of a star on the plate and in the sky, we can solve for the tangent point. In general, there can be zero, one or two solutions. Here we use the star we just found. The high-level wrapper erfa.tpors treats the status code as an error, so we call the low-level function from erfa.ufunc, which returns both solutions and the number of solutions:

import erfa.ufunc

az1, bz1, az2, bz2, n = erfa.ufunc.tpors(x / fl, y / fl, ra, dec)
print("number of solutions:", n)

for az, bz in [(az1, bz1), (az2, bz2)][:n]:
    sign, t = erfa.a2tf(2, az)
    sign, d = erfa.a2af(1, bz)
    print(f"tangent point = {t['h']:02d} {t['m']:02d} {t['s']:02d}.{t['f']:02d}  "
          f"{sign.decode()}{d['h']:02d} {d['m']:02d} {d['s']:02d}.{d['f']}")
number of solutions: 2
tangent point = 01 29 03.48  +88 29 44.5
tangent point = 09 00 00.00  +89 30 00.0

Two pointings of the astrograph would have put that star at that position of the plate: one is the true pointing we started with (9h, +89° 30') and the other is on the other side of the pole. This ambiguity can only happen close to a celestial pole. Over most of the sky, and for small fields, there is exactly one solution.

Summary

  • The chain of astrometry is: catalog (ICRS) → astrometric → GCRS → CIRS → observed. For most uses, three functions are enough: atci13, atio13 and atco13, and their inverses.
  • Give the proper motion as \(d\alpha/dt\), not as \(\mu_\alpha\cos\delta\); the parallax in arcseconds; the time in TT or UTC as each function asks; and do not forget UT1−UTC.
  • For many stars, fill the context once (apci13, apco13) and transform with atciq and atioq, that accept arrays. To track in time, update only the Earth rotation with aper13.
  • The functions without the "13" let you use your own ephemerides, CIP corrections and models. atciqn adds planets to the light deflection.
  • Every step (pmpx, ldsun, ab, the BPN matrix, atio13) can also be called by itself, as in Example 5.
  • The tangent-plane functions tpxes, tpsts, tpors link the sky with the images on a detector.
