SOFA: Miscellaneous Topics

The previous chapters covered the big subjects. SOFA also contains a few smaller, self-contained groups of functions. Most of the time a short description and the name of the function are all you need, so in this chapter each topic gets a few lines and a short example.

All the examples of this page start from the same few lines:

import numpy as np
import erfa

DD2R, DR2D, DAS2R, DMAS2R = erfa.DD2R, erfa.DR2D, erfa.DAS2R, erfa.DMAS2R

Ecliptic coordinates

The ecliptic is the plane of the Earth's orbit around the Sun, and ecliptic longitude and latitude \((\lambda, \beta)\) are natural for the Solar System. Classically one converts from equatorial coordinates by a single rotation about the x axis by the obliquity of the ecliptic. But since the IAU resolutions of 2000 the ecliptic is no longer a basis for precise work, and the ICRS replaces the mean J2000.0 system. SOFA offers three functions, all based on the IAU 2006 precession. They combine the mean equinox and ecliptic of date (for \(\lambda, \beta\)) with the ICRS (for \(\alpha, \delta\)):

  • eqec06(date1, date2, dr, dd): ICRS right ascension and declination to ecliptic longitude and latitude \((\lambda, \beta)\).
  • eceq06(date1, date2, dl, db): the inverse.
  • ecm06(date1, date2): the rotation matrix. If you have many positions to transform, compute it once and use rxp.

The date is given in TT as a two-part Julian date. As an example, we compute the ecliptic coordinates of Regulus (ICRS \(\alpha = 10^h 08^m 22.311^s\), \(\delta = +11^\circ 58' 01.95''\)), which is very close to the ecliptic, on two dates twenty-four years apart. The longitude increases by about 0.33°, because of precession:

ra  = erfa.tf2a("+", 10, 8, 22.311)
dec = erfa.af2a("+", 11, 58, 1.95)

for year in (2000, 2024):
    tt1, tt2 = erfa.dtf2d("TT", year, 1, 1, 12, 0, 0.0)
    lon, lat = erfa.eqec06(tt1, tt2, ra, dec)
    print(f"{year}: ecliptic longitude = {lon * DR2D:8.4f} deg, latitude = {lat * DR2D:7.4f} deg")

# the inverse brings us back
ra2, dec2 = erfa.eceq06(tt1, tt2, lon, lat)
print("round trip error:", abs(ra2 - ra) / DAS2R, "arcsec")
2000: ecliptic longitude = 149.8291 deg, latitude =  0.4648 deg
2024: ecliptic longitude = 150.1644 deg, latitude =  0.4662 deg
round trip error: 0.0 arcsec

Galactic coordinates

Galactic longitude and latitude \((l, b)\) are based on the plane of our Milky Way, and Sagittarius A* (the centre of the Galaxy) is close to \(l = 0\), \(b = 0\). The old IAU 1958 definition has some problems, and SOFA does not try to reproduce it exactly: it uses a rotation matrix like the one published with the Hipparcos catalog. There are two functions, for ICRS \(\leftrightarrow\) galactic: icrs2g(dr, dd) and g2icrs(dl, db).

# Sagittarius A*, the radio source at the center of the Milky Way (ICRS)
ra  = erfa.tf2a("+", 17, 45, 40.04)
dec = erfa.af2a("-", 29, 0, 28.1)
l, b = erfa.icrs2g(ra, dec)
print(f"Sgr A*: l = {l * DR2D:.3f} deg, b = {b * DR2D:+.3f} deg")

# where is the north galactic pole in the ICRS?
ra_ngp, dec_ngp = erfa.g2icrs(0.0, np.pi / 2)
print(f"North galactic pole: RA = {ra_ngp * DR2D:.3f} deg, Dec = {dec_ngp * DR2D:+.3f} deg")
Sgr A*: l = 359.944 deg, b = -0.046 deg
North galactic pole: RA = 192.859 deg, Dec = +27.128 deg

Hipparcos and FK5

The FK5 was the standard star catalog before the ICRS. The Hipparcos catalog, which realizes the ICRS, differs from it, mostly in two ways: the whole FK5 system is rotated with respect to the Hipparcos system by about 32 mas, and it slowly spins by about 1 mas per year. Five SOFA functions convert catalog entries (positions, proper motions, parallax and radial velocity) between the two:

  • h2fk5 and fk52h: Hipparcos entry ↔ FK5 entry.
  • hfk5z and fk5hz: the same for stars for which the proper motion, the parallax and the radial velocity are all unknown (they need the date).
  • fk5hip: gives the orientation and the spin in the form that the other functions use.

The proper motions are in radians per year as \(d\alpha/dt\), and the parallax in arcseconds, as in all SOFA functions. Let us take a star and see how its FK5 values differ from the Hipparcos ones (the star is the one that we met in the Astrometry chapter), and also the orientation and spin of the FK5 relative to Hipparcos:

rh = erfa.tf2a("+", 14, 34, 16.81183)
dh = erfa.af2a("-", 12, 31, 10.3965)
drh = np.arctan2(-354.45e-3 * DAS2R, np.cos(dh))      # d(alpha)/dt in rad/yr
ddh = 595.35e-3 * DAS2R
pxh, rvh = 0.16499, 0.0

r5, d5, dr5, dd5, px5, rv5 = erfa.h2fk5(rh, dh, drh, ddh, pxh, rvh)
print(f"position difference FK5 - Hipparcos:  {(r5 - rh) * np.cos(dh) / DMAS2R:6.2f} mas in RA*cos(dec), "
      f"{(d5 - dh) / DMAS2R:6.2f} mas in Dec")
print(f"proper-motion difference (mas/yr):    {(dr5 - drh) * np.cos(dh) / DMAS2R:6.2f}, {(dd5 - ddh) / DMAS2R:6.2f}")

# orientation (a rotation matrix) and spin (an r-vector in rad/yr) of the FK5 relative to Hipparcos
orientation, spin = erfa.fk5hip()
print("spin of FK5 (mas/yr):", np.round(spin / DMAS2R, 2))
position difference FK5 - Hipparcos:   26.96 mas in RA*cos(dec),   5.29 mas in Dec
proper-motion difference (mas/yr):      0.65,   0.66
spin of FK5 (mas/yr): [-0.3  0.6  0.7]

FK4 and FK5

Before the FK5 (1980s), positions were published in the FK4 system, usually for the equinox B1950.0. The conversion to modern coordinates is more than a precession from 1950 to 2000. The precession model itself changed, the practice of including part of the annual aberration in the catalog positions (the E-terms) was abandoned, and the system of proper motions was corrected for the rotation of the Galaxy. If you read a position published before 1984 and need J2000.0 coordinates, do not just precess it: use these functions.

  • fk425: FK4 (B1950.0) entry → FK5 (J2000.0) entry.
  • fk45z: the same for a star with unknown proper motion, parallax and radial velocity; it needs the Besselian epoch of the observation.
  • fk524 and fk54z: the opposite direction.
# a position from an old catalog, B1950.0: 10h 00m 00s, +30 00 00; epoch of observation 1950.0
r1950 = erfa.tf2a("+", 10, 0, 0.0)
d1950 = erfa.af2a("+", 30, 0, 0.0)

r2000, d2000 = erfa.fk45z(r1950, d1950, 1950.0)

sign, t = erfa.a2tf(2, r2000)
sign2, d = erfa.a2af(1, d2000)
print(f"J2000.0: {t['h']:02d} {t['m']:02d} {t['s']:02d}.{t['f']:02d}  "
      f"{sign2.decode()}{d['h']:02d} {d['m']:02d} {d['s']:02d}.{d['f']}")

# for comparison: only the IAU 1976 precession, without the FK4 particularities
d1, d2 = erfa.epb2jd(1950.0)
rp = erfa.pmat76(d1, d2)                       # precession matrix J2000.0 -> B1950.0
v = erfa.trxp(rp, erfa.s2c(r1950, d1950))      # use its transpose to go from 1950 to 2000
ra_p, dec_p = erfa.c2s(v)
print(f"precession alone differs by {erfa.seps(ra_p, dec_p, r2000, d2000) / DAS2R:.2f} arcsec")
J2000.0: 10 02 52.73  +29 45 29.0
precession alone differs by 0.28 arcsec

The position moved by about two-thirds of a degree in 50 years, mostly because of the precession of the equinoxes. A conversion with the precession alone differs from the correct one by 0.28 arcseconds, which is small for the eye but huge compared with the precision of modern astrometry (milliarcseconds). This is the effect of the E-terms and the other peculiarities of FK4.

Geodesy: the shape of the Earth

Many calculations need the position of the observer, and a sphere is a poor model of the Earth: an oblate spheroid (an ellipsoid flattened at the poles) is 2 or 3 orders of magnitude better. SOFA supports only this level; for the finer effects (tides, the real surface) you need other software.

  • eform(n) gives the equatorial radius \(a\) (in meters) and the flattening \(f = (a-b)/a\) of a standard ellipsoid: erfa.WGS84 (1), erfa.GRS80 (2) or erfa.WGS72 (3).
  • gd2gc(n, elong, phi, height) converts geodetic coordinates to the geocentric Cartesian vector (in meters). gc2gd does the opposite. The versions gd2gce and gc2gde take the parameters \(a\) and \(f\) of any ellipsoid.

Here, geodetic latitude is the angle between the equator and the line normal to the ellipsoid (what a map, a GPS and a plumb line give), while geocentric latitude is the angle seen from the centre of the Earth. They differ by up to 0.19° at mid-latitudes. The height is measured above the ellipsoid. Let us convert the position of the observing site used in the Astrometry chapter (Strasbourg) and see the difference:

for n in (1, 2, 3):
    a, f = erfa.eform(n)
    print(f"ellipsoid {n}: a = {a:.1f} m, 1/f = {1 / f:.6f}")

elong = 7.744083817548831 * DD2R
phi = 48.58313582900411 * DD2R
height = 140.0

xyz = erfa.gd2gc(1, elong, phi, height)                  # WGS84
print("geocentric vector (m):", np.round(xyz, 2))

lon, lat, h = erfa.gc2gd(1, xyz)                         # and back
print(f"back to geodetic: lon = {lon * DR2D:.8f}, lat = {lat * DR2D:.8f}, height = {h:.3f} m")

geocentric_lat = np.arctan2(xyz[2], np.hypot(xyz[0], xyz[1]))
print(f"geodetic latitude   = {phi * DR2D:.4f} deg")
print(f"geocentric latitude = {geocentric_lat * DR2D:.4f} deg")
ellipsoid 1: a = 6378137.0 m, 1/f = 298.257224
ellipsoid 2: a = 6378137.0 m, 1/f = 298.257222
ellipsoid 3: a = 6378135.0 m, 1/f = 298.260000
geocentric vector (m): [4188848.49  569636.73 4760123.36]
back to geodetic: lon = 7.74408382, lat = 48.58313583, height = 140.000 m
geodetic latitude   = 48.5831 deg
geocentric latitude = 48.3921 deg

Solar System ephemerides

An ephemeris gives the positions of the bodies of the Solar System at any time. SOFA has very little in this area. High-precision ephemerides (such as the ones of the JPL) have no official IAU status and they need big data files, which does not match the idea of a self-contained library. Only three tools, all without IAU status, are provided:

  • epv00(date1, date2): the position and velocity of the Earth, heliocentric and barycentric, with an accuracy of about 10 km and 5 mm/s. It is based on a shortened version of the VSOP2000 planetary theory, and is used by many other SOFA functions (for example, for aberration and parallax).
  • moon98(date1, date2): the geocentric position and velocity of the Moon, accurate to about 20 km (some arcseconds for an observer on Earth). Good for visibility, rise and set times, but not for eclipses and occultations.
  • plan94(date1, date2, np): the heliocentric position and velocity of the planets. The number np selects the body: 1 Mercury, 2 Venus, 3 the Earth-Moon barycentre, 4 Mars, 5 Jupiter, 6 Saturn, 7 Uranus, 8 Neptune. It is very approximate: a few arcseconds for Mercury and Venus and up to a few arcminutes for the outer planets. Fine for a "planetarium" program.

The dates are given as two-part Julian dates in TDB (TT can be used instead, with no visible effect). All three functions return a pv-vector: a structured NumPy value with the position 'p' in au and the velocity 'v' in au/day. Let us see the distance from the Earth to the Sun at the Earth's closest and farthest points of 2024, the distance to the Moon, and the distance of Jupiter from the Sun:

AU_KM = erfa.DAU / 1000          # one au in km

for label, (year, month, day) in [("perihelion time", (2024, 1, 3)), ("aphelion time  ", (2024, 7, 5))]:
    utc1, utc2 = erfa.dtf2d("UTC", year, month, day, 12, 0, 0.0)
    tai1, tai2 = erfa.utctai(utc1, utc2)
    tt1, tt2 = erfa.taitt(tai1, tai2)

    pvh, pvb = erfa.epv00(tt1, tt2)                       # the Earth
    earth_sun = np.linalg.norm(pvh["p"])                  # au

    moon = erfa.moon98(tt1, tt2)                          # geocentric Moon
    earth_moon = np.linalg.norm(moon["p"]) * AU_KM        # km

    jupiter = erfa.plan94(tt1, tt2, 5)                    # heliocentric Jupiter
    sun_jupiter = np.linalg.norm(jupiter["p"])            # au

    print(f"{year}-{month:02d}-{day:02d} ({label}): Earth-Sun = {earth_sun:.4f} au, "
          f"Earth-Moon = {earth_moon:,.0f} km, Sun-Jupiter = {sun_jupiter:.3f} au")
2024-01-03 (perihelion time): Earth-Sun = 0.9833 au, Earth-Moon = 402,775 km, Sun-Jupiter = 4.985 au
2024-07-05 (aphelion time  ): Earth-Sun = 1.0167 au, Earth-Moon = 385,296 km, Sun-Jupiter = 5.028 au

The Earth is closest to the Sun (0.983 au) in early January and farthest (1.017 au) in early July, as expected. The Moon's distance changes during the month between about 357,000 and 406,000 km, so the two values simply show where the Moon was on those two dates. For better planetary positions, use a JPL ephemeris, for example through the package numeph (in the section "Python Packages" of this website) or through astropy.

Summary

  • Ecliptic: eqec06, eceq06, ecm06 (ICRS ↔ mean ecliptic of date).
  • Galactic: icrs2g, g2icrs.
  • Hipparcos ↔ FK5: h2fk5, hfk5z, fk52h, fk5hz, fk5hip.
  • FK4 ↔ FK5: fk425, fk45z, fk524, fk54z. Never convert B1950 positions by precession alone.
  • Geodesy: eform, gd2gc, gc2gd, gd2gce, gc2gde.
  • Ephemerides: epv00 (Earth), moon98 (Moon), plan94 (planets). They are all approximate.
