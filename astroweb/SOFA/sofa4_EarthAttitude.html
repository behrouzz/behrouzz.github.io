SOFA: Tools for Earth Attitude

The Earth is not a fixed platform. Its axis slowly changes direction, wobbles a little, and the planet spins at a speed that is not perfectly constant. To say where a star is in the local sky of an observer, or to know where on the Earth's surface a satellite is passing above, we must know the orientation (the attitude) of the Earth at the moment of interest. This chapter explains how SOFA describes that orientation and how to compute it with pyerfa.

Where is the star? A short tour of coordinate systems

To specify the direction of a star we need to answer three questions: what type of coordinates, which reference axes (where is the pole and where is longitude zero), and which observer. The reference axes are the part that concerns us here.

Precession and nutation

You know right ascension \(\alpha\) and declination \(\delta\): they are the longitude and latitude on a sphere based on the Earth's rotation axis. The zero point of right ascension was classically the equinox, the point where the Sun crosses the celestial equator going north. But the picture is complicated because the Sun, the Moon and the planets pull on the Earth's equatorial bulge, and so neither the equator nor the ecliptic (the plane of the Earth's orbit) are fixed. The consequence is that the \(\alpha\) and \(\delta\) of a star change all the time, even if the star itself does not move.

  • Precession is the slow, large part. The Earth's axis draws a cone around the pole of the ecliptic in about 26,000 years. The equinox moves along the ecliptic by roughly 50 arcseconds each year.
  • Nutation is the collection of small periodic wobbles on top of precession. The biggest term has a period of 18.6 years, with an amplitude of the order of ten arcseconds.

These two are in reality parts of one phenomenon, separated only because their sizes are so different. The models come from a mixture of theory and observation and are improved from time to time. The nutation model of 1980 (IAU 1980) has 106 terms in each of the two angles, while the modern one (IAU 2000A) has 1365 terms, some smaller than a tenth of a microarcsecond.

Old and new reference systems

With the textbook system (equator and equinox of date) it was hard to do high-precision work, because both the equator and the ecliptic were moving and the ecliptic itself was not clearly defined. At the beginning of the 21st century the International Astronomical Union (IAU) changed the recommended procedures in four ways:

  1. A fixed reference system. The International Celestial Reference System (ICRS) has axes fixed with respect to distant extragalactic objects. It does not depend on the motion of the Earth at all, so a star has ICRS coordinates that change only because of its own motion. The ICRS was oriented so that it coincides with the old J2000.0 mean equator and equinox system within about 25 milliarcseconds, only for continuity. Two companions describe the same axes for different observers: the Barycentric (BCRS) and the Geocentric (GCRS) celestial reference systems.
  2. No more ecliptic as the basis for precise work.
  3. Earth rotation angle instead of sidereal time. The Earth Rotation Angle (ERA) is a simple linear function of UT1 only. Sidereal time was a complicated function of both UT1 and time.
  4. A new zero point of right ascension: the Celestial Intermediate Origin (CIO) replaces the equinox. The CIO is a point on the equator which is defined by its motion: it moves as little as possible along the equator (it only moves north and south as the equator tilts). In the 20th and 21st centuries it stays within 0.1 arcsecond of the GCRS right ascension zero.

The result is that the sequence of coordinate systems that connect a star in the ICRS to what the observer sees is the following:

Read the diagram from left to right and from top to bottom. Each box is a reference system, with the variables that describe a direction in it, and each arrow is a correction or a transformation. The first row is the work of the astrometry chapter: from the ICRS catalog entry, the space motion gives the BCRS (the star's direction as seen from the barycentre of the Solar System at the date of observation), the parallax gives the astrometric place, and light deflection and aberration give the geocentric direction, the GCRS. The rest of the diagram, from the GCRS to the ITRS, is what this chapter is about. Here the route splits into the classical and the modern branch, which join again at the TIRS. (In the astrometry chapter we use the modern branch only.) Note that for the TIRS and the ITRS the coordinates are a terrestrial longitude and latitude.

The old route, with the equinox and sidereal time, is still supported by SOFA for old applications. It replaces CIRS by the geocentric apparent place and the Earth rotation angle by the Greenwich apparent sidereal time (GST). SOFA's advice is clear: use the CIO-based method for every new application.

Some important quantities

  • Frame bias. The ICRS pole and the J2000.0 mean pole are not exactly the same. The pole of J2000.0 sits at \(X = -0.016617''\), \(Y = -0.0068192''\) in the ICRS and the mean equinox lies 0.0146'' west of the ICRS meridian. This tiny rotation (23 mas in total) is the frame bias.
  • The CIP. The Celestial Intermediate Pole is the instantaneous pole of the Earth, as moved by precession and nutation. In the modern method it is given by just the two numbers \(X\) and \(Y\): the first two components of its unit vector in the GCRS.
  • The CIO locator \(s\). It tells where the CIO is located on the CIP equator. It is tiny (about −0.002 arcseconds in 2007), and SOFA computes it with a series. (More exactly, SOFA models \(s + XY/2\) and then subtracts the second term.)
  • The TIO locator \(s'\). The same idea on the terrestrial side, for the Terrestrial Intermediate Origin (TIO). It is smaller than 0.1 mas even after two centuries, so it's often ignored. The function is sp00.
  • The equation of the origins (EO). It is the angular distance between the CIO and the equinox, along the equator. It is what links the new and old systems: \(EO = ERA - GST\). It changes by about 46 arcseconds per year, mainly because of precession. If you have to support both methods, work with the CIO and use the EO to convert.

The hour angle \(h\) is computed in the same way in both systems, \(h = \theta - \alpha\) (plus the observer's east longitude), where \(\theta\) is the Earth rotation angle (or the sidereal time in the old system) and \(\alpha\) is the right ascension (CIRS or apparent, respectively). Both give the same hour angle. A very important warning: in the old system the sidereal time model must match the precession-nutation model. Mixing an old and a new model gives hour angle errors of about 0.05 arcseconds. In the CIO-based method, there is no such trap.

From the sky to the ground: the whole chain as one equation

Frame bias, precession-nutation, Earth rotation and polar motion are four rotations, applied one after the other. In matrix form, a direction written in the GCRS becomes a direction in the ITRS by:

$$\mathbf{v}_{ITRS} = R_{PM}\; R_3(\theta)\; R_{NPB}\; \mathbf{v}_{GCRS}$$

where \(R_{NPB}\) is the matrix for bias, precession and nutation, \(R_3(\theta)\) is a rotation about the z axis by the Earth rotation angle (or by GST if \(R_{NPB}\) is equinox based) and \(R_{PM}\) is the polar-motion matrix. The SOFA function c2t06a computes exactly this complete product for you.

The SOFA functions

SOFA supports several generations of models. Here we list them with the pyerfa names. Do not try to learn this list: it is a map for later reference.

Precession and obliquity

  • IAU 1976 (Fricke-Lieske): prec76 (the three Euler angles) and pmat76 (the matrix).
  • IAU 2000: pr00 (corrections to the precession rates), bi00 (frame bias), pmat00 (precession+bias matrix), bp00 (bias and precession matrices separately).
  • IAU 2006 (Capitaine et al.): pfw06 gives the four Fukushima-Williams angles \(\gamma, \phi, \psi, \epsilon\) which include frame bias, and pmat06 gives the matrix. A neat advantage of this form is that the nutation angles can simply be added to the two last angles.
  • Mean obliquity of the ecliptic: obl80 (IAU 1976) and obl06 (IAU 2006).

Nutation

  • nut80 (IAU 1980), nut00a (IAU 2000A, the best) and nut00b (IAU 2000B, a short version with 1 mas accuracy in 1995–2050). Each returns the nutation in longitude \(\Delta\psi\) and in obliquity \(\Delta\epsilon\).
  • nut06a is IAU 2000A with the small adjustments needed to be consistent with the IAU 2006 precession.
  • From the two angles, numat builds the nutation matrix. In one step: nutm80, num00a, num00b, num06a.

The CIP, the CIO and polar motion

  • The coordinates \(X, Y\) of the CIP: bpn2xy (from a bias-precession-nutation matrix), xys00a and xys00b (also return \(s\)), fw2xy (from Fukushima-Williams angles), and xy06 (directly from a series).
  • The CIO locator \(s\): s00, s00a, s00b, s06, s06a. The versions without a letter at the end are faster when you already have \(X, Y\).
  • Polar motion: pom00 builds \(R_{PM}\) from the pole coordinates \(x_p, y_p\) (published by the IERS) and \(s'\), which comes from sp00.

Earth rotation

  • Earth rotation angle: era00. That is all you need for the modern method.
  • Greenwich mean sidereal time: gmst82, gmst00, gmst06.
  • Equation of the equinoxes: eqeq94, ee00, ee00a, ee00b, ee06a, plus eect00 for the small "complementary terms".
  • Greenwich apparent sidereal time \(GST = GMST + EE\): gst94, gst00a, gst00b, gst06, gst06a.

The "fundamental arguments"

The nutation series and the series for \(X, Y\) and \(s\) are written in terms of 14 angles which depend on time: the Delaunay variables (fal03, falp03, faf03, fad03, faom03), the mean longitudes of the planets from Mercury to Neptune (fame03, fave03, fae03, fama03, faju03, fasa03, faur03, fane03) and the general precession fapa03. You will not need them directly.

The current models and the high-level functions

The most recent models rest on the IAU 2006 precession (in the Fukushima-Williams form), the IAU 2000A nutation with its 2006 adjustments, the IERS 2003 polar motion and the IAU 2000 Earth rotation angle. Almost all functions whose names end in 06 or 06a use the same set of models, so they agree with each other. This is what you should use:

  • c2i06a(tt1, tt2): the CIO-based \(R_{NPB}\) matrix (GCRS to CIRS). Its transpose, from tr, goes back.
  • pnm06a(tt1, tt2): the equinox-based matrix (GCRS to true equator and equinox of date).
  • pfw06 + nut06a + fw2m: the same, but step by step. Or fw2xy + s06a + c2ixys, which gives you the chance to add the IERS corrections to \(X, Y\).
  • era00(ut11, ut12) for the Earth rotation angle, or gst06a(ut11, ut12, tt1, tt2) for the sidereal time.
  • c2t06a(tt1, tt2, ut11, ut12, xp, yp): the complete GCRS to ITRS matrix in a single call. The lower-level c2tcio and c2teqx build the same matrix from the parts.
  • eo06a: the equation of the origins; eors: the EO from a given matrix and \(s\).
  • pb06: the old three precession angles \(\zeta, z, \theta\) that include the frame bias (for old software).

Example 1: the pieces, one by one

Before the long example, let us compute the main ingredients of the Earth's attitude for a single date: 2007 April 5 at 12:00:00 UTC. For the UT1 we need the difference UT1−UTC from the IERS, which was −0.072073685 s on that day. (The numbers used in this chapter are for illustration, but they are close to the real ones.)

import numpy as np
import erfa

np.set_printoptions(precision=12, suppress=True)

DAS2R, DMAS2R, DR2D = erfa.DAS2R, erfa.DMAS2R, erfa.DR2D

# the date: UTC -> TAI -> TT, and UTC -> UT1
utc1, utc2 = erfa.dtf2d("UTC", 2007, 4, 5, 12, 0, 0.0)
tai1, tai2 = erfa.utctai(utc1, utc2)
tt1, tt2 = erfa.taitt(tai1, tai2)

dut1 = -0.072073685                 # UT1-UTC in seconds (IERS)
ut11, ut12 = erfa.utcut1(utc1, utc2, dut1)

print(f"TT  = {tt1} + {tt2:.15f}")
print(f"UT1 = {ut11} + {ut12:.15f}")
TT  = 2454195.5 + 0.500754444444444
UT1 = 2454195.5 + 0.499999165813831

Now the Fukushima-Williams precession angles (which already include frame bias), the nutation and the obliquity:

gamb, phib, psib, epsa = erfa.pfw06(tt1, tt2)
dpsi, deps = erfa.nut06a(tt1, tt2)
print(f"gamma_bar = {gamb / DAS2R:12.6f} arcsec")
print(f"phi_bar   = {phib / DAS2R:12.6f} arcsec")
print(f"psi_bar   = {psib / DAS2R:12.6f} arcsec")
print(f"eps_A     = {epsa / DAS2R:12.6f} arcsec")
print(f"nutation in longitude = {dpsi / DAS2R:.6f} arcsec")
print(f"nutation in obliquity = {deps / DAS2R:.6f} arcsec")
print(f"mean obliquity        = {erfa.obl06(tt1, tt2) * DR2D:.6f} deg")
gamma_bar =     0.715857 arcsec
phi_bar   = 84378.015524 arcsec
psi_bar   =   365.661663 arcsec
eps_A     = 84378.006566 arcsec

nutation in obliquity = 9.284462 arcsec

The angle \(\bar\gamma\) is less than one arcsecond, and \(\bar\psi\) is the accumulated precession in longitude: 366 arcseconds, about 6 arcminutes, which is what we expect for about 7 years at 50 arcseconds per year. The angles \(\bar\phi\) and \(\epsilon_A\) are both very close to the obliquity of the ecliptic, 84378 arcseconds, which is 23.44°. The nutation is at the level of a few arcseconds. Next, the Earth rotation angle, the sidereal times, and the equation of the origins:

era  = erfa.era00(ut11, ut12)                   # depends on UT1 only
gmst = erfa.gmst06(ut11, ut12, tt1, tt2)        # needs UT1 and TT
gst  = erfa.gst06a(ut11, ut12, tt1, tt2)
eo   = erfa.eo06a(tt1, tt2)

print(f"ERA  = {era * DR2D:.9f} deg = {era / 15 * DR2D:.9f} h")
print(f"GMST = {gmst * DR2D:.9f} deg")
print(f"GST  = {gst * DR2D:.9f} deg")
print(f"EO   = {eo / DAS2R:.6f} arcsec")
print(f"ERA - GST = {(era - gst) / DAS2R:.6f} arcsec")

GMST = 13.411485802 deg
GST  = 13.412416949 deg
EO   = -338.126340 arcsec

The last two lines show that \(EO = ERA - GST\), as we said. The EO is only about −338 arcseconds, i.e. the equinox is about 5.6 arcminutes away from the CIO on this date.

Finally, the matrix that rotates GCRS coordinates into CIRS coordinates and the CIP coordinates it contains:

rc2i = erfa.c2i06a(tt1, tt2)
x, y = erfa.bpn2xy(rc2i)
s = erfa.s06(tt1, tt2, x, y)
print(rc2i)
print(f"X = {x:.12f}  Y = {y:.12f}  s = {s / DAS2R:.6f} arcsec")

The matrix is very close to the identity (the diagonal is almost 1), as the Earth's axis has moved only a little since 2000. The third row is the unit vector of the CIP, which is \((X, Y, \sqrt{1-X^2-Y^2})\).

Example 2: from the celestial to the terrestrial frame, in five ways

Now the main example of the SOFA cookbook. The task is to calculate the complete matrix \(R_{GCRS\to ITRS}\) for the date above, with five different methods. The first is the classical, pre-2003 one; the others are the successive generations of the IAU models. We also need the Earth orientation parameters, which are measured and published by the IERS. All of these values are for illustration:

xp = 0.0349282 * DAS2R              # polar motion (arcsec -> rad)
yp = 0.4833163 * DAS2R

ddp80 = -55.0655 * DMAS2R           # corrections to the IAU 1980 nutation
dde80 = -6.3580 * DMAS2R

dx00 = 0.1725 * DMAS2R              # corrections to the CIP, IAU 2000A (mas -> rad)
dy00 = -0.2650 * DMAS2R

dx06 = 0.1750 * DMAS2R              # corrections to the CIP, IAU 2006/2000A
dy06 = -0.2259 * DMAS2R

def diff_uas(a, b):
    """Size of the rotation between two matrices, in microarcseconds."""
    return np.linalg.norm(erfa.rm2v(erfa.rxr(a, erfa.tr(b)))) / DAS2R * 1e6

Method 1: IAU 1976/1980/1982/1994 (equinox based)

This is how it was done before 2003. We build the precession matrix and the nutation matrix, multiply them, and use the Greenwich apparent sidereal time as the rotation angle. The old polar motion matrix of the IERS 1996 convention is just two small rotations.

rp = erfa.pmat76(tt1, tt2)                      # IAU 1976 precession, J2000.0 to date
dp80, de80 = erfa.nut80(tt1, tt2)               # IAU 1980 nutation
dpsi, deps = dp80 + ddp80, de80 + dde80         # add the observed corrections
epsa = erfa.obl80(tt1, tt2)                     # mean obliquity
rn = erfa.numat(epsa, dpsi, deps)               # nutation matrix
rnpb = erfa.rxr(rn, rp)                         # N x P

ee = erfa.eqeq94(tt1, tt2) + ddp80 * np.cos(epsa)           # equation of the equinoxes
gst = erfa.anp(erfa.gmst82(ut11, ut12) + ee)                # apparent sidereal time

rc2ti = erfa.rz(gst, rnpb)                      # celestial -> terrestrial intermediate

rpom = erfa.ir()                                # polar motion (IERS 1996)
rpom = erfa.rx(-yp, rpom)
rpom = erfa.ry(-xp, rpom)
rc2it_1 = erfa.rxr(rpom, rc2ti)

print("GST =", gst * DR2D, "degrees")
print(rc2it_1)

Method 2: IAU 2000A, CIO based

Since 2003 the IAU 2000A model is the standard. We take the CIP coordinates \(X, Y\) and \(s\) from xys00a, add the observed corrections, build the GCRS-to-CIRS matrix with c2ixys, then rotate by the Earth rotation angle. For the polar motion, the new convention needs the TIO locator \(s'\).

x, y, s = erfa.xys00a(tt1, tt2)
x, y = x + dx00, y + dy00                       # add corrections to the CIP

rc2i = erfa.c2ixys(x, y, s)                     # GCRS -> CIRS
era = erfa.era00(ut11, ut12)                    # Earth rotation angle
rc2ti = erfa.rz(era, rc2i)                      # CIRS -> TIRS

sp = erfa.sp00(tt1, tt2)                        # TIO locator
rpom = erfa.pom00(xp, yp, sp)                   # polar motion matrix
rc2it_2 = erfa.rxr(rpom, rc2ti)

print(f"X = {x:.15f}, Y = {y:.15f}, s = {s / DAS2R:.9f} arcsec")
print("ERA =", era * DR2D, "degrees")
print(rc2it_2)
print(f"difference with method 1: {diff_uas(rc2it_1, rc2it_2):.2f} microarcsec")

Method 3: IAU 2000A, equinox based

Equinox-based applications can use the new nutation too. But now the corrections are given as \((dX, dY)\) for the CIP, so we must convert them into corrections of nutation \((d\Delta\psi, d\Delta\epsilon)\) first. Also, it is necessary to choose the sidereal time and equation of the equinoxes functions that match the IAU 2000 precession: gmst00 and ee00.

dp00, de00 = erfa.nut00a(tt1, tt2)
epsa, rb, rp, rpb, rn, rnpb = erfa.pn00(tt1, tt2, dp00, de00)

# convert the CIP corrections (dX, dY) into nutation corrections
v1 = np.array([dx00, dy00, 0.0])
v2 = erfa.rxp(rnpb, v1)
ddp00 = v2[0] / np.sin(epsa)
dde00 = v2[1]

dpsi, deps = dp00 + ddp00, de00 + dde00
rn = erfa.numat(epsa, dpsi, deps)
rnpb = erfa.rxr(rn, rpb)

gst = erfa.anp(erfa.gmst00(ut11, ut12, tt1, tt2) + erfa.ee00(tt1, tt2, epsa, dpsi))
rc2ti = erfa.rz(gst, rnpb)

rpom = erfa.pom00(xp, yp, erfa.sp00(tt1, tt2))
rc2it_3 = erfa.rxr(rpom, rc2ti)

print("GST =", gst * DR2D, "degrees")
print(rc2it_3)
print(f"difference with method 2: {diff_uas(rc2it_3, rc2it_2):.2f} microarcsec")

Method 5: IAU 2006/2000A, CIO based, from the X,Y series

Example 3: what is above my head?

The matrix \(R_{GCRS\to ITRS}\) is a rotation. Applied to the direction of a star, it gives the direction as seen in the Earth-fixed frame. A direction in the ITRS has a longitude and a latitude on the Earth: that is the point of the Earth's surface that has this star right above it, at the zenith.

Let us do this for Sirius (ICRS coordinates 06h 45m 08.917s, −16° 42' 58.02") on the date of the example. We ignore the proper motion, the parallax and the aberration, which would change the answer by only some arcseconds, and use the matrix of method 4:

ra  = erfa.tf2a("+", 6, 45, 8.917)
dec = erfa.af2a("-", 16, 42, 58.02)

v_gcrs = erfa.s2c(ra, dec)                      # unit vector in the GCRS
v_itrs = erfa.rxp(rc2it_4, v_gcrs)              # the same direction in the ITRS

lon, lat = erfa.c2s(v_itrs)
lon = erfa.anpm(lon)                            # east longitude, -180..+180 degrees

print(f"Sirius is at the zenith at longitude {lon * DR2D:.3f} deg (east), "
      f"latitude {lat * DR2D:.3f} deg")
Sirius is at the zenith at longitude 87.956 deg (east), latitude -16.721 deg

At 12:00 UTC on that day, Sirius is straight overhead for an observer in the Indian Ocean, at about 88° east and 16.7° south. Because the Earth turns, this point moves west by 15° each hour. And the same matrix, transposed, brings you back from the ground to the sky: erfa.trxp(rc2it_4, v_itrs).

Example 4: the pole in the far future

The standard precession models are very good for a few centuries around the present. For much longer times, the polynomial models fail. SOFA includes a special long-term precession model (Vondrák et al. 2011), valid for about 200,000 years on either side of the year 2000. With the function ltpequ we can ask: where will the Earth's rotation axis point in 12,000 years?

for epoch in (2000.0, 14000.0):
    pole = erfa.ltpequ(epoch)                   # unit vector of the equator pole, J2000.0 frame
    ra_p, dec_p = erfa.c2s(pole)
    print(f"year {epoch:7.1f}: RA = {np.degrees(ra_p) / 15 % 24:6.3f} h, Dec = {np.degrees(dec_p):+7.3f} deg")

# bright star Vega, ICRS
ra_v  = erfa.tf2a("+", 18, 36, 56.3)
dec_v = erfa.af2a("+", 38, 47, 1.3)
ra_p, dec_p = erfa.c2s(erfa.ltpequ(14000.0))
print(f"distance from Vega: {erfa.seps(ra_p, dec_p, ra_v, dec_v) * DR2D:.1f} deg")
year  2000.0: RA =  6.000 h, Dec = +90.000 deg
year 14000.0: RA = 18.399 h, Dec = +45.140 deg
distance from Vega: 6.8 deg

In the year 2000 the axis points almost exactly to the north celestial pole (declination +90°, near Polaris). In the year 14,000 the pole of the equator will be about 45° away from the present pole (its declination in the current frame will be +45°), pointing to a place only about seven degrees from the bright star Vega. Vega will be the pole star of that time. The other long-term functions give the precession matrix (ltp, ltpb) and ecliptic quantities (ltpecl, ltecm, lteceq, lteqec).

Summary

  • The orientation of the Earth is described by four rotations: frame bias, precession-nutation, Earth rotation and polar motion.
  • The modern way uses the CIP (\(X, Y\)), the CIO locator \(s\) and the Earth rotation angle. The old way uses the equinox and the sidereal time. The equation of the origins connects them.
  • For new programs use the IAU 2006/2000A functions: c2i06a, era00, eo06a, or the complete c2t06a.
  • Earth orientation parameters (UT1−UTC, polar motion, CIP corrections) come from the IERS; they cannot be predicted by a model.
  • All the different approaches agree to within a fraction of a milliarcsecond.
